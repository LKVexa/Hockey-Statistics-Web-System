The scorer's language

The language is a tri-token line language: a team, a player and a stat, with modifiers. It is the same in both editions and in every client of the reference edition, because one lexer and one compiler serve them all.

Line forms

TEAM PLAYER STAT[/mod…]One event. PLAYER is the player's four-letter code, J91, 0091 or 91 (the jersey)
LINE TEAM P1 … P6The skaters on the ice for TEAM; a goalie's code sets the crease. A team with a LINE is tracked by shifts from then on ({{ref:lineups}})

The four-letter words of the language — LINE UNDO STOP NEXT GAME HELP EXIT QUIT — can never be team codes, so a line's first word always says what kind of line it is.

Events

An event is the most common line. The stat token says what happened; the team and player say who did it. There are {{n_tokens}} stat tokens: the specification's G S M B A A1 A2 ZE ZX H TK GV FOW FOL P2 P4 P5 P10 P20 PS IN OUT, and two declared extensions that HK001's inputs need, FC (forecheck attempt) and OMR (odd-man rush). Appendix B lists every token with its meaning and the HK001 inputs it feeds.

FOCO SMIT G/2P          a goal by SMIT from zone 2, snap shot
FOCO HALA A             an assist: bound to FOCO's open goal (primary, then secondary)
KELW BARK B             a blocked shot, logged for the blocking player
FOCO SMIT FOW/N         a faceoff won in the neutral zone (KELW's centre loses it automatically)
KELW WITC ZE/C          a controlled zone entry
FOCO 19 S/78,-4         a shot by jersey 19 at x = 78 ft, y = −4 ft
KELW BARK P2/TRP        a two-minute minor for tripping

Modifiers follow the stat after a slash. The compiler writes them in a canonical order (1W, never W1) and the actor checks them against the same grammar, so a frame carries one spelling of each event.

The clock

A new game's clock stands still at 20:00 of the first period, and nothing a scorer types — setting the clock, a lineup, a faceoff, a shot — makes it run. It runs only from the game's start frame:

LineEffect
T mm:ss [period]Sets the scoreboard clock without starting it: T 20:00 P1 before a game, T 12:31 to correct it. A clock time is at most 20:00 and a period at most 12
STARTThe start frame: the opening faceoff. The clock runs from this line, from the time it shows (20:00 P1 unless T set another). A game has one start frame; a second START is refused and names the moment the game started
GORestarts the clock after a stoppage or at the start of a later period. In a game created in this edition, GO before START is refused: "the game has not started: type START at the opening faceoff"
WHISTLE [mm:ss]The whistle: stops the running clock, and play is dead until the faceoff. WHISTLE 12:34 also sets the clock to the time the official clock showed when the whistle went, which takes back the moment between the whistle and the keystroke; typed after a whistle has already stopped the clock, it sets that whistle's time, and after STOP it makes the stoppage a whistle. In the workbench Esc is the whistle ({{ref:input-assist}})
STOP · NEXT · ENDStops the clock without a whistle · moves to the next period of the game's overtime rules, stopped at its full length ({{ref:overtime}}) · ends the game

The start frame is a frame like any other line: it is recorded in the ledger (CLOCK START in Q AUDIT), and the clock history begins with it. The rule applies to every game created in this edition, by rink new and by rink sim (whose synthetic games are started with START); a game created by an earlier build has no start frame and starts with its first GO, as it always did, so its ledger replays unchanged.

A whistle is refused when there is no play for it to stop — before the game has started, before the clock has run in the period, in the shootout — and, without a time, when the clock already stands still (the clock is already stopped at P2 12:33; GO restarts it · WHISTLE mm:ss sets the time the whistle went). A whistle's time is at most the period's length. Every whistle is a frame in the ledger (CLOCK WHISTLE, or CLOCK WHISTLE 12:34, in Q AUDIT); the reply counts the period's whistles (P2 12:34 ⏸ · whistle 6 of P2), the scoreboard counts the game's and names the last, and the score bar shows ⏸ whistle while the clock stands at one. A whistle that sets the clock back after a line was already entered behaves as T setting it back: the stint in progress ends where play had reached, and a new one starts at the whistle's time.

The clock history is piecewise, and every event takes the clock at its first keystroke, not at Enter: a scorer who starts typing a goal at 14:02 and finishes at 13:58 records the goal at 14:02. Events typed while the clock stands still take the time it shows.

Overtime rules

When regulation ends level, a game is decided by its overtime rules. A league sets them with rink overtime, and every game created for the league's teams keeps a copy of them from its creation: the home team's league's, or the away team's when the home team is in none. A game keeps the rules it was created under whatever the league changes later, so a change applies to the games created after it, and every recorded game replays exactly as it was played. A game outside any league with rules, and every game created by an earlier build, keeps the built-in rules.

The rules have two blocks: the regular block decides regular-season and preseason games, the playoff block playoff and championship games. Each block has three settings.

Setting (regular · playoff)ValuesMeaning
ot= · playoff-ot=0, or 1:00 to 20:00The overtime period's length; 0 is no overtime, in the regular block only (a playoff game must be decided)
skaters= · playoff-skaters=3, 4 or 5Skaters a side in overtime
shootout= · playoff-shootout=0, or 1 to 10Shootout rounds after the overtime, then sudden death; 0 is no shootout

The clock commands, the shootout, the strength and the result all follow the rules:

  • Periods. P1 to P3 are regulation, 20:00 each. Period 4 is the overtime (OT) of the block's length; period 5 is the shootout (SO) when there is one. With no overtime, NEXT at the end of P3 goes straight to the shootout and T … OT is refused. A playoff block without a shootout plays sudden-death periods of its length until a goal: OT1 to OT9, periods 4 to 12.
  • Sudden death. A goal in overtime ends the game. The shootout ends as soon as one side can no longer be caught within its rounds, or when the rounds are complete with one side ahead; level after them, it goes on round by round.
  • Ties. Where the block has no shootout and no further overtime, a game still level at the end of its last period ends tied: NEXT or END there makes it final, TIE. A tie earns each side one point, and the standings add a T column.
  • Strength. Overtime played 3 or 4 a side starts at that strength. A penalty takes a skater from the penalized side down to three, then gives the other side one more, up to five: 3 on 3 becomes 4 on 3, then 5 on 3; 4 on 4 becomes 4 on 3, then 5 on 3. The scoreboard's strength and every stint follow it.
  • Refusals. A clock command the rules do not allow is refused and says why: no period 4: this game ends after P3, and a tie stands; the shootout decides the game; type the attempts, or END.

The built-in rules are those of every earlier build: a regular-season game plays a 5:00 3-on-3 sudden-death overtime and a shootout of three rounds; a game of any other stage, preseason included, plays 20:00 sudden-death periods until a goal, up to period 12. They equal standard except in preseason, where a league's rules apply the regular block. rink new names the rules a new game keeps — overtime: 5:00 3-on-3 OT, shootout 3 (MTNL rules) — and the scoreboard carries them as overtime. Setting a league's rules needs the administrative capability, which the operator holds in this edition.

Lineups

LINE TEAM P1 … P6 names a team's players on the ice; a goalie's code among them sets the crease. A team with a line is tracked by shifts: every change of a line rotates the stint ({{ref:stints}}). IN and OUT events record single changes. A line of more than three players is carried by two system frames (!LIN and !LIA), applied together.

Corrections

A correction never overwrites anything: it is a frame of its own, appended to the ledger, and the game's state is folded again from the start. The audit trail therefore shows the event and its correction.

LineEffect
UNDOWithdraws the last event this operator entered in this game ("nothing of yours to undo" when there is none)
DEL nWithdraws event number n of this game
FIX n TEAM PLAYER STAT[/mods]Replaces event n with the event given
FIX n A1=19 A2=44 XY=78,-4Patches fields of event n: A1= and A2= the assists of a goal, P= player, T= team, S= stat, M= modifier, X= Y= or XY= coordinates; - clears (A2=-, XY=-, M=-)

A correction is checked as a new line is: coordinates on the ice, at most four letters or digits in M=, and the patched event still valid. A FIX that would make its event invalid — an unknown player, a modifier the stat does not take, a team that is not playing, an assist by the scorer, an assist on an event that is not a goal — is refused and nothing changes. FIX cannot turn an event into an assist or a shift; DEL it and enter it again.

Queries

Q TARGET answers from the game state. In RINK mode it reads the selected game unless told otherwise; outside it, q box and the like take the same targets and flags. Each target accepts only its own flags and refuses any other, so a mistyped filter never returns an unfiltered answer (Appendix C).

The common flags are --game=G1 or --season, --team=FOCO, --str= (ALL, EV, PP, ST or a skater count such as 5v5), --min= minutes and --n= rows. Counts are whole numbers, clamped to their range.

Locale

LOCALE fr-CA enables the specification's French aliases, which compile to the canonical token before a frame is made: B (but) is G, T (tir) is S, MG and MP are FOW and FOL, and so on (Appendix B). The frame, and therefore the ledger, holds the canonical token only.

One line, all or nothing

All frames of one line are applied atomically: if any is rejected, those of the line already applied are rolled back, and the line answers with the reason. Frames are idempotent by their UUIDv7: a frame sent twice answers DUPLICATE with the original outcome and event number. A frame is client bytes, so decoding is strict: every text field must be canonical (A–Z and 0–9, NUL-padded), coordinates both present or both absent and on the ice, and a frame that fails is rejected as malformed without echoing what it held ({{ref:strict-decoding}}).
